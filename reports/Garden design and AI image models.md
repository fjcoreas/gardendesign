# Keep the house, redesign the garden

The project works best as two linked jobs. The first is to design the way professionals do: interview the homeowner, analyse the site, settle zones and structure, and only then choose plants. The second is to render that design with an engineered pipeline rather than a chat prompt. As of November 2026, the best fit for a weekend build is **Nano Banana 2.1 called through the Gemini API, not the Gemini app**, wrapped in a segment, edit and composite pipeline that **pastes the original pixels back over the house, walls, mature trees and sky**. No instruction-based image model guarantees that untouched regions stay untouched, and compositing makes that guarantee by construction. The fallback is **OpenAI's GPT Image 2.5/2.6 edit endpoint with a true binary mask**. It leads the independent image-editing leaderboards and costs about the same per edit. Mistral is not an option: it has **no image model of its own**, and Le Chat's image editing runs on Black Forest Labs' FLUX models. The user's mediocre results in the Gemini app most likely came from a mix of causes: the app's lower download resolution, drift across chained edits, the lack of any mask or composite step, and short, keyword-style prompts. Model quality was probably not the main problem. At roughly **$0.03–0.11 per image**, a weekend of testing costs about $5–20. One caveat runs through everything below. Researchers could not open most source pages because of a network allowlist, so the report rests largely on search-result summaries. Model IDs, prices, leaderboard scores and several design dimensions need checking against primary sources before the project relies on them. The final section lists which figures matter most.

## Professionals interview, survey and zone before choosing a single plant

Residential garden design follows a remarkably stable sequence across the UK and US, even though firms name the stages differently. UK studios describe following the Society of Garden Designers' work stages, which are modelled on architects' stages, and note that "not all projects require all stages" ([Stephen Woodhams Garden Design](https://www.stephenwoodhams.com/design-process)). US practice, as framed by APLD and university extension services, runs client interview, site inventory and analysis, base plan, concept, preliminary design and master plan ([APLD](https://www.apld.org/landscape-design-definition); [UF/IFAS EP375](https://edis.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/EP375)). The first meeting usually happens on site. John Brookes' studio described it as a visit that "will ascertain your requirements and enable an inspection and assessment of your garden" ([John Brookes](https://www.johnbrookes.com/process.asp)). The designer then writes a **brief for the client to sign off**, which practitioners call "a vital document upon which the design is based" ([Lucy Roberts](https://www.lucyroberts.uk.com/process.html)).

After the brief come a measured survey (1:50 or 1:100) and site analysis ([Garden UK](https://gardenuk.co.uk/garden-design/working-with-garden-designer-process-uk/)). Next is a concept stage that "shows the layout without committing to specific materials or planting" ([Garden Ninja](https://gardenninja.co.uk/garden-design-process)). Then comes a scaled master plan with construction notes ([Sitescape Design](https://sitescapedesign.com/about-us)). The **planting plan comes last**: a schedule listing botanical and common names, quantities and spacing ([Elite Service Solutions](https://www.eliteservicesolutions.co.uk/blog/whats-actually-included-in-garden-design-package/)). Aftercare closes the job, with a tailored maintenance plan ([Butler and Parker](https://www.butlerandparker.com/process)).

The philosophy behind the sequence comes from Brookes, who held that a design should rest "first and foremost on the needs of its occupants" ([Wikipedia: John Brookes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Brookes_(landscape_designer))). Kew-trained designer Richard Rogers put the practical consequence bluntly: "For most designers the plants come last in the design process after the layout and structure of the garden has been decided" ([Gardens Illustrated](https://www.gardensillustrated.com/garden-design/garden-design-mistakes-avoid)).

For this project, the sequence translates into an eight-step method. Steps one to three are intake with photos, a conversational interview that produces a one-page brief, and site analysis from the photos plus the answers. Step four produces two or three zoning concepts. Step five fixes the chosen layout, step six writes the planting plan and schedule, step seven sets phasing and budget, and step eight produces a maintenance calendar. Professionals present **two or three options, each with a plain explanation of what it solves**, and limit early feedback to big decisions ([iScapeit](https://www.iscapeit.com/blog/how-landscape-pros-can-help-clients-understand-design-ideas-faster)).

Designers move from brief to layout through zoning and bubble diagrams. Zoning places broad public, private and service territories. Bubbles map functions such as patio, lawn, vegetable beds and shed, and the relationships between them. One guide calls the relationship of each space "to the house and to the other functional areas" the "single most important consideration at this stage" ([Garden Design Exposed](https://www.gardendesignexposed.com/garden_plan.html); [Pretty Purple Door](https://www.prettypurpledoor.com/landscape-bubble-drawing/)). The implication for the AI pipeline is direct: **generate images only after zoning is agreed**, so each render illustrates a coherent plan instead of improvising a makeover. A text mood board makes a cheap, checkable substitute for a visual board and doubles as the prompt basis. It needs style words, 3–5 hard materials, a colour palette and 8–12 signature plants.

When budgets are tight, designers still complete a master plan first, because phasing without one is "a common and costly mistake". Trees planted before the patio is fixed may have to be dug up ([Houzz](https://www.houzz.com/magazine/what-you-should-know-about-a-phased-landscape-design-project-stsetivw-vs~165314353)). Grading belongs in phase one because it is cheapest done all at once ([NALP](https://blog.landscapeprofessionals.org/strategic-spaces-navigating-the-design-build-process-for-phased-landscape-projects/)). The most-used area, usually the terrace off the house, comes next, often as a smaller version of the final footprint built in better materials ([John Montgomery Landscape Architects](https://jmontgomerydesigns.com/design-on-a-budget/)). For an older homeowner, safety and access items such as level paths, rails and lighting should be phased ahead of aesthetics.

### What photos reveal and what the homeowner must tell us

A professional site appraisal records soil type, contour, aspect, microclimate, exposure and drainage. It also covers frost pockets, rain shadows and wind tunnels, good and bad views, and overhead and underground services ([Hortigraph, RHS R2111](https://hortigraph.uk/rhs/r-2111-2-1/)). Good photos reveal a lot of this. Wide shots from the house and from the far end of the garden show layout, boundaries, hardscape condition, level changes, existing trees and shrubs, views to frame or screen, and the house's materials. They also show visible services such as drain covers and taps, and symptoms of problems: mossy lawn suggests poor drainage or shade, bare soil under trees suggests dry shade.

Photos cannot reliably give compass orientation, climate, soil chemistry, drainage behaviour, wind, buried services or legal constraints. **Orientation is the single most important missing fact.** One method is to stand at the back door with a compass and track sun across the day ([Garden Ninja](https://www.gardenninja.co.uk/garden-aspects-explained/)). Climate maps to the RHS H1a–H7 hardiness ratings ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/trials-awards/award-of-garden-merit/rhs-hardiness-rating)) or to the 2023 USDA zone map, which runs on average about 2.5°F warmer than its predecessor ([USDA ARS](https://www.ars.usda.gov/news-events/news/research-news/2023/usda-unveils-updated-plant-hardiness-zone-map/)). Drainage can be tested by filling a hole at least 0.5 m deep with water and timing how long it takes to drain ([Konzept Garden](https://konzeptgarden.com/blogs/news/landscape-site-analysis-checklist)).

The interview should confirm what photos suggest rather than ask about it cold. Published questionnaires from UK studios, US firms and trade bodies converge on the themes below ([Melissa Morton](https://melissamorton.co.uk/garden-design-questionnaire/); [My Garden Design](https://mygardendesign.co.uk/building-a-garden-design-brief); [Southern Landscape Group](https://www.tslg.org/blog/tslg-landscape-design-questionnaire/); [Fine Gardening](https://www.finegardening.com/article/6-questions-garden-designers-ask-before-they-plan-a-new-garden); [Todd Haiman](https://www.toddhaimanlandscapedesign.com/gardening-resources/10-questions-you-should-ask-before-designing-a-garden)). The order shown lets the conversation flow, and the questions are our synthesis of those sources.

| Theme | Questions to ask (P = partly answerable from photos; confirm instead of asking) | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Site facts (ask first) | Town or postcode; coastal or inland. Which way does the back of the house face, and where does the sun set? Morning, midday and evening sun on each area, summer vs winter (P). Soil: clay, sandy, chalky? Do rhododendrons thrive locally? Does water sit after rain (P)? Wind, frost pockets. Buried pipes, cables, septic tank. Rules: tree protection, conservation area, HOA, fire zone, watering bans | Drives every plant choice and flags things photos cannot show |
| People and use | Who uses the garden (grandchildren's ages, pets)? Activities: relaxing, dining (for how many?), hobby gardening, food, drying washing, storage, compost. Where and when does she like to sit, in sun or shade? What is seen from the kitchen and living-room windows? Routes to bins, shed, gate | Brookes' principle: use drives design |
| Feel and style | Formal or relaxed? Words for the feel. Pictures she loves. Colours she likes and dislikes. How full should it look? How should it relate to the house? | Selects the style lens and colour scheme |
| Must-keeps and dislikes | What she loves now and what must stay, including sentimental plants (P). Eyesores, overlooking, hated chores. Loved and hated plants. Allergies, thorny or toxic plants to avoid | Defines the keep mask and the plant blacklist |

The questionnaire should finish the way professionals do: **summarise the brief back in about a page and ask "Have I got this right?"** before any design work begins. Experts warn against overwhelming clients with exhaustive forms ([Houzz Pro Learn](https://pro.houzz.co.uk/pro-learn/blog/startup-guide-interior-design-how-to-write-client-questionnaire-with-template)), so the AI should skip questions the photos already answer.

## Eighteen checks separate a designed garden from an amateur one

The principles below are the rulebook's core. Where sources supply numbers, the numbers are given, along with where sources disagree. Defaults the notes synthesised are labelled as our rules.

### Structure first, scaled to the house

The best-documented proportioning method is **Brookes' grid**. Take a module from the house, such as bay width, door and window spacing or room width, extend it across the plot, and draw terraces, paths and beds on that grid. This ties the garden's horizontals to the building's verticals ([Denmans Garden](https://www.denmans.org/about/john-bio-page/)). At The Old Rectory, Wiggonholt, Brookes used the house's 9 m recessed bay to set the terrace proportions ([Google Arts & Culture / Garden Museum](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/john-brookes-garden-museum/SAUBdyEKB6xdIA?hl=en)).

Thomas Church's four principles add the rest of the frame. They are unity of house and garden, function, simplicity ("a simple layout is more beautiful and effective than a fussy one"), and scale, meaning parts that relate "in a comfortable proportion of size" ([Wikipedia: Thomas Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Church_(landscape_architect)); [Serenity in the Garden](https://serenityinthegarden.blogspot.com/2017/04/thomas-church-and-his-gardens-for-people.html)).

The golden ratio appears in blog advice, but the evidence for it is weak and inconsistent ([gardenuk.co.uk](https://gardenuk.co.uk/garden-design/garden-design-principles-beginners-uk/)). Treat it as a loose heuristic: rectangles of roughly 1:1.6 or multiples of the house module look settled.

The design order follows from "structure first". Settle the ground plane and hardscape first, then boundaries and hedges, then trees and evergreen shrubs, and finally perennials, grasses and bulbs. Evergreens "hold the garden's shape in winter" ([gardenuk.co.uk](https://gardenuk.co.uk/garden-design/garden-design-principles-beginners-uk/)). The often-quoted "one-third evergreen" rule is folk wisdom: blogs variously give 20–30%, a third, or as much as 60% ([gardendesignapp.ai](https://gardendesignapp.ai/planting/evergreen-structure)). Our default is about 25–35% evergreen or woody structure. Our check is a winter test: mentally delete every herbaceous plant and ask whether defined spaces, edges, a focal point and evergreen masses remain.

### Restraint and repetition create unity

Designers' most consistent warning is against cramming. In small gardens people "overcompensat[e]... by trying to cram in more things", when fewer, larger, layered beds work better ([Homes & Gardens](https://www.homesandgardens.com/gardens/small-garden-mistakes-and-how-to-fix-them)). Plants grouped in threes or other odd numbers, with each cluster repeated two or three times, create rhythm ([Kane Landscapes](https://kanelandscapes.com/blog-rule-of-3-garden-design/)). A starter matrix palette is about 20 taxa: 3 structural plants, 5 seasonal plants in waves and 2 matrix species ([Plant Wild](https://plantwild.com/garden-design/how-to-use-matrix-planting-designs/)). Our rules are 2–3 hard materials plus one accent, echoing the house, and about 10–20 plant taxa for a small or medium garden. Keep one style vocabulary per space.

### Every main view ends in something deliberate

"A common landscape design mistake is to forget the view from inside the house", and gardens without focal points become "visually chaotic and confusing" ([Garden Design magazine](https://www.gardendesign.com/landscape-design/common-mistakes.html)). Good off-site views should be framed and borrowed ([Garden Ninja](https://www.gardenninja.co.uk/using-borrowed-views-and-focal-points-in-garden-design/)). A counterintuitive rule applies to eyesores: a big tree or shrub planted directly in front of one "often has the opposite effect of drawing the eye towards the area" ([Gardens Illustrated](https://www.gardensillustrated.com/features/major-garden-eyesores-how-to-hide)). Screens work better close to the viewer, or a stronger focal point can redirect the eye. Our rule is to identify the kitchen window, living-room window and back door as the primary viewpoints, give each one focal point on a sightline, and never place more than one competing focal point in a view.

### Generous dimensions, real destinations

Amateur terraces and paths are almost always too small. The most consistent recommendation for a main path is **about 1.2 m**, enough for two people abreast or a wheelbarrow ([Surrey Oak Landscapes](https://surreyoaklandscapes.co.uk/blog/how-to-plan-garden-path)). Secondary paths are 600–750 mm ([GreatGardenPlans](https://greatgardenplans.com/how-wide-should-garden-paths-be/)), plus 100–300 mm on each side where planting will flop over ([Homes & Gardens](https://homesandgardens.com/gardens/garden-path-design-rules)). HGTV's narrower 2–3 ft for main paths is an outlier ([HGTV](https://www.hgtv.com/gardening/landscaping-and-hardscaping/designing-paths-for-your-landscaping)).

A dining terrace for four to six needs **at least about 3 × 3 m** ([Homebuilding & Renovating](https://homebuilding.co.uk/advice/patio-sizes)), and 3.5–4 m is better. Outdoor steps follow Church's comfort formula, twice the riser plus the tread, targeting about 650 mm ([NParks CUGE](https://www.nparks.gov.sg/-/media/cuge/pdf/rtn-02-2009---steps-in-the-landscape)). Risers of about 140 mm are "easier and safer when wet" ([Bunnings Workshop](https://www.workshop.bunnings.com.au/discussion/341331)). Every path must end at a destination, and every curve needs a reason.

### Planting design: layers, density, seasons

Modern planting design layers a structural layer, a seasonal-theme layer, a groundcover matrix and dynamic fillers, so that soil is covered at maturity ([Wikipedia: Matrix planting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_planting)). Density is contested. Noel Kingsbury favours **7–9 perennials per m²** ([Noel Kingsbury](https://www.noelkingsbury.com/noelsgarden-blog/2019/6/20/mind-the-gap)), while RHS-derived calculators give about 5 per m² ([gardencalc.uk](https://www.gardencalc.uk/planting/plant-spacing-calculator)). A rule of 5–9 per m² covers both.

Warm colours advance and cool colours recede. A long, thin garden therefore reads deeper with hot colours near the house and cool colours at the far end ([gardenuk.co.uk](https://gardenuk.co.uk/garden-design/using-colour-garden-design-uk/)). Planning only for spring and summer is "one of the most common mistakes" ([Homes & Gardens](https://homesandgardens.com/gardens/common-spring-garden-design-mistakes-and-how-designers-fix-them)). Our rule is at least three seasons of interest per bed, with spacing set for mature size.

### Maintenance and sustainability as design drivers

Dense groundcover suppresses weeds ([Meristem Horticulture](https://meristemhorticulture.com/planted/layer-by-layer)). Well-planted gravel gardens are low maintenance and need little watering ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/garden-inspiration/design/wild-and-wonderful-garden)). The RHS also calls a pond, "however tiny", the single easiest wildlife win, and prefers hedges to fences as wildlife corridors ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/wildlife/in-the-garden/encourage-wildlife-to-your-garden)). It recommends minimal, directional lighting on timers. In England, impermeable front-garden paving over 5 m² that does not drain to a permeable area needs planning permission ([UK Government](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a797a8eed915d07d35b5d8e/pavingfrontgardens.pdf)). A lawn is worth keeping only if it gets used ([Living Etc](https://www.livingetc.com/news/the-5-garden-design-mistakes-people-make-and-how-to-avoid-them)).

### Designing for a homeowner who may age in place

Falls happen at steps and sudden level changes, so accessible design minimises both and uses firm, non-slip, level surfaces ([Gardening Etc](https://www.gardeningetc.com/design/accessible-garden-design)). Loose bark is "uneven & unstable – not wheel-able; tripping hazard" ([City of Vancouver](https://vancouver.ca/files/cov/Community-Gardens-Accessibility-Guidelines-2011.pdf)). Ramps of 1:12 are commonly cited, but one source calls that too steep in practice and prefers 1:20 ([Flowerpotman](https://www.flowerpotman.com/gardening-for-the-elderly-and-disabled/garden-design-for-the-elderly/)). Seats every 6–9 m with shade make the garden usable longer ([Grand Millennial](https://grandmillennial.net/garden-design-for-aging-in-place-layout-tools-and-safety/)). Raised beds at **71–81 cm** put soil at desk height ([Gardening Know How](https://www.gardeningknowhow.com/garden-how-to/info/future-proof-garden-for-aging)), although other sources give 60 cm or 70–80 cm.

### Edit an existing garden before erasing it

The consensus for inherited or established gardens is to "resist clearing anything you're not certain about" for a full growing cycle, while dealing with hazards immediately ([Ian Green Garden Design](https://www.iangreengardendesign.co.uk/post/inherited-a-garden-here-s-why-you-shouldn-t-rush-in); [BBC Gardeners' World](https://www.gardenersworld.com/how-to/grow-plants/inheriting-a-garden/)). Some wildlife, such as bats, is illegal to disturb ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/garden-inspiration/garden-types/mature-gardens)). For this project, mature trees, sound walls and established evergreens give structure that takes 10–20 years to replace. The design should classify every existing element as keep, edit or remove, and prefer reshaping beds and re-routing paths to wholesale clearance.

The checklist below turns the sourced mistakes into automatic checks the AI runs against every proposed design ([Gardens Illustrated](https://www.gardensillustrated.com/garden-design/garden-design-mistakes-avoid); [Garden Design magazine](https://www.gardendesign.com/landscape-design/common-mistakes.html); [Homes & Gardens](https://www.homesandgardens.com/gardens/small-garden-mistakes-and-how-to-fix-them)). The threshold values are our defaults, synthesised from the figures above.

## Fifteen designers become practical style lenses

The canon divides into three families. The naturalistic and ecological planters are Oudolf, Pearson, Stuart-Smith, Chatto, Filippi, Rainer and West, and the Sheffield school. The structure-first designers are Caruncho, Giubbilei, Maynard, Sturgeon and Burle Marx. The romantic colourists are Jekyll, Lloyd, Don and Price.

For an image model, a lens is only useful if it has a renderable signature, so the table pairs each sourced philosophy with prompt cues and an upkeep rating. **The plant palettes and prompt cues are the research team's synthesis from general horticultural knowledge and were not verified against primary sources.** The upkeep ratings are unverified estimates. Two figures in the canon died in 2026. Nigel Dunnett died on 26 April 2026 ([University of Sheffield](https://sheffield.ac.uk/architecture-landscape/news/loving-memory-professor-nigel-dunnett)). Fernando Caruncho's death was reported around August 2026 but is unconfirmed ([Gardens Illustrated](https://www.gardensillustrated.com/features/fernando-caruncho-in-memory)).

All follower counts are third-party snapshots. They should shape tone and accessibility, not design authority.

For the user's mother, the evidence points to **Chatto gravel (or Filippi in a dry climate), Rainer-and-West groundcover communities, or a simple formal frame with naturalistic infill**. These lenses keep the garden looking deliberate while cutting watering, weeding and mowing. The Maynard and Stuart-Smith model of clipped evergreen structure around loose planting is the most versatile single lens, because the frame keeps the garden reading as intentional even when the planting relaxes. Jekyll and Lloyd borders, extensive topiary and large lawns belong on the menu only if paid help is available.

The model landscape has moved past the names most guides still cite. **Gemini 2.5 Flash Image, the original "Nano Banana", retired on 2 October 2026.** Imagen 3 mask editing is discontinued, and developers note there is "no mask-based editing replacement" ([Vertex AI release notes](https://docs.cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/docs/release-notes); [Google developer forum](https://discuss.google.dev/t/imagen-3-0-capability-001-retiring-june-30-no-mask-based-editing-replacement-exists/343602)). Google's current general image model is **Nano Banana 2.1**, reported as released on 6 October 2026 at about half the cost of its predecessor ([Unite.AI](https://www.unite.ai/nano-banana-2-1-debuts-at-half-the-image-cost-of-its-predecessor/)). On independent rankings, OpenAI leads. Arena's single-image edit board (12 November 2026, about 31.9M votes) placed **GPT-Image-2.5 Sunburst first at 1519** ([Arena](https://arena.ai/leaderboard/image-edit)). Artificial Analysis' AA-Image-Editing v2.1 placed **GPT Image 2.6 Sunburst first at 1210** ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/image/leaderboard/editing)). Nano Banana 2.1 reportedly sits about 50 Elo behind GPT Image 2.6 Sunburst on Arena (1427 vs 1476), although that figure was not attributed to a single page ([OrcaRouter](https://www.orcarouter.ai/blog/gpt-image-2-6-vs-nano-banana-2-1)). When Artificial Analysis rebuilt its arena into 17 categories in about September 2026, **Seedream 5.0 Pro led identity-preserving edits**. That is the category closest to "keep the house, change the garden" ([superpowerdaily.com](https://superpowerdaily.com/posts/artificial-analysis-rebuilds-image-editing-arena-with-different-leaders-by-task)). Arena votes reward visual appeal and instruction-following, not pixel preservation, and neither board has a landscape or exterior category. The rankings are therefore a partial proxy at best.

The preservation evidence favours OpenAI, but thinly. In one side-by-side blog test, Nano Banana 2.1 was asked to change only the background and also swapped shorts for jeans, while GPT Image 2.6 changed nothing else ([Picsart](https://picsart.com/blog/gpt-image-2-6-sunburst-vs-nano-banana-2-1)). Community reports describe a pink or magenta cast on Nano Banana Pro and Nano Banana 2 edits at 2K and 4K ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/edited-images-return-with-a-slight-pink-color-cast-after-migration-from-preview-to-image/176315)). They also describe repeating tiled backgrounds at 4K ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/4k-image-generation-produces-repeating-tiled-background-artifacts-after-migration-from-preview-to-image/175782)), which third parties say 2.1 fixed ([Kenerate AI](https://kenerateai.com/model/nano-banana-2.1)). No published benchmark measures pixel-level preservation outside the edit region for any 2026 model. Even OpenAI regenerates the full frame rather than compositing, so it cannot promise pixel-identical walls either.

That evidence settles the recommendation. **The primary model is Nano Banana 2.1 through the Gemini API.** It is cheap enough for heavy iteration, accepts up to 14 reference images for cross-view consistency, outputs up to 4K, and fits the user's existing Google access. Its known weaknesses are drift outside the edit area, colour cast and size changes, and the composite step neutralises all three. **The fallback is GPT Image 2.5/2.6 `images.edit` with a binary mask**, for views where Gemini keeps altering structure or the quality gap shows. **FLUX.1 Fill [pro]** is the deterministic option for a single stubborn region. A single fal.ai key reaches Nano Banana 2.1, GPT Image 2, FLUX and Qwen, which makes a weekend A/B comparison easy from one Python client ([fal.ai](https://fal.ai/nano-banana-2.1)).

Mistral adds nothing here. Le Chat's image generation comes from a BFL partnership, and its editing feature uses FLUX Kontext ([Mistral](https://mistral.ai/news/le-chat-dives-deep/); [The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/mistral-ai-adds-deep-research-voice-mode-image-editing-and-more-to-le-chat/)). Going directly to BFL is strictly more controllable. Avoid the retired Gemini 2.5 Flash Image, the shut-down Imagen editing, and Adobe's Firefly API, which reportedly requires enterprise contracts ([sudomock.com](https://sudomock.com/blog/adobe-firefly-api-pricing-2026)).

## Compositing, not prompting, guarantees the house stays put

The commercial AI garden apps fail exactly where this project must succeed. App Store reviewers complain that apps "change the original house or office building you import" and "can't keep your actual landscape in the design, instead replacing it with fake, generic images". One reviewer got tropical plants for Northern Virginia ([App Store: Garden AI](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/garden-ai-landscape-design/id6738805390?see-all=reviews); [App Store: AI Garden Design](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/ai-garden-design-landscape-ai/id6744179402?see-all=reviews&platform=ipad)). The fix is architectural, not linguistic. A developer write-up on Nano Banana 2.1 advises **masking or compositing with the original photo whenever content must stay pixel-exact**, because reference-based generation cannot guarantee it ([dev.to](https://dev.to/drew_grant_7c81ce9e84426a/nano-banana-21-treat-image-edits-like-testable-specs-3do1)). A 2026 arXiv survey recommends an explicit non-edit mask plus a copy-paste fallback for unmodified regions ([arXiv 2604.28185](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.28185)).

The pipeline has five stages. No source describes this exact pipeline for gardens; it is the research team's assembly of proven components.

**Stage one normalises the photo**: apply EXIF rotation, convert to sRGB and record the dimensions. Converting iPhone Display P3 images to sRGB avoids some colour shifts ([apiyi docs](https://docs.apiyi.com/en/faq/banana-pro-edit-red-cast)). **Stage two builds a KEEP mask** with **SAM 3**, which now sits in Hugging Face Transformers and turns short text prompts such as "house", "wall", "fence", "sky" and "tree" into binary masks ([Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/docs/transformers/model_doc/sam3)). Without a GPU, it runs on CPU, Colab or Roboflow's hosted endpoint ([Roboflow](https://blog.roboflow.com/segment-anything-with-text/)). A quick overlay lets the homeowner approve what counts as fixed. **Stage three edits**: Claude writes the design spec and a per-view prompt. For Gemini, the editable region is marked on the image and described in the prompt; for OpenAI or FLUX Fill, the inverted mask goes in directly. **Stage four composites.** Resize the output back to the original dimensions and optionally colour-match it to the original inside the KEEP region (mean and standard-deviation matching in LAB space), which corrects any pink cast. Then alpha-composite the original pixels over the KEEP mask, slightly eroded and feathered with a 5–15 px Gaussian blur to hide seams. **Stage five extends the design to other views** by feeding the first approved render, plus the shared design spec, as references when editing the other photos.

This is about **200–400 lines of Python** (google-genai or openai SDK, transformers, Pillow or OpenCV, numpy) plus a Gradio or Streamlit front end with a before/after slider and the plant list. That is feasible in a weekend, though the line count is the research team's estimate, not a measured figure.

Three practical problems deserve attention up front. The first is that tree canopies and fences against planting produce ragged mask edges. The second is that new planting at the foot of a wall must overlap the wall's base, so the KEEP mask needs a deliberately carved "planting band" along the bottom of walls and the house. Otherwise the composite will slice new shrubs in half. The third is that Gemini may subtly shift geometry, which would show as seams at the mask boundary. Feathering helps, and if it does not, that view should go to the masked OpenAI fallback.

The same pipeline explains the user's disappointing app results. The Gemini app reportedly downloads at 1K without a paid plan and 2K with one ([Medium review](https://medium.com/@leucopsis/nano-banana-pro-googles-gemini-3-pro-image-model-a-review-11cbaee32ee1)). It offers no mask or composite step and encourages chained conversational edits. Academic work shows such edits accumulate JPEG-style noise and global colour shifts turn by turn ([arXiv 2512.01755](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.01755)), and a late-2026 hands-on reports the same for Nano Banana 2.1 ([BigGo Finance](https://finance.biggo.com/news/9c87ca6e-67c8-4ff2-92d3-ec6bff899c8a)). The API, or AI Studio for no-code testing, exposes resolution up to 4K, ten or more aspect ratios and up to 14 references ([Google Cloud Blog](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/ultimate-prompting-guide-for-nano-banana)).

There is no free API tier for the Nano Banana models, although AI Studio offers some free use ([developersdigest.tech](https://www.developersdigest.tech/models/image-generation/google-gemini-api)). Whether a consumer Google AI subscription includes API credit was not established. For quick no-code experiments in the app itself, its draw-on-image feature lets users circle the beds to change ([Tom's Guide](https://www.tomsguide.com/ai/ai-image-video/nano-banana-now-lets-you-draw-prompts-directly-on-your-photos-sam-altman-is-going-to-hate-this)). A related trick also works through the API: draw coloured boxes and text instructions onto a copy of the photo, then ask the model to "read the green text in the image and make the modifications. Remove the green text and boxes" ([Jim MacLeod](https://jimmacleod.medium.com/use-canva-to-guide-nano-banana-a-simple-step-by-step-tutorial-9e2df0c7455a)).

## Gemini prompts should read like a designer's brief, not a wish list

Google's guidance has stayed consistent across model versions. Write in full descriptive sentences rather than keyword lists, start with a strong verb that names the operation, and frame things positively ("empty street" rather than "no cars"). Name camera, lens and lighting, and refine conversationally ([Google Cloud Blog](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/ultimate-prompting-guide-for-nano-banana)). Google calls its edit mode "text-based semantic masking": the model edits part of an image and leaves the rest untouched when told explicitly what must stay the same. Its official inpainting template changes only one named element "and keep[s] everything else in the image exactly the same, preserving the original style, lighting and composition" ([Google Developers Blog](https://developers.googleblog.com/how-to-prompt-gemini-2-5-flash-image-generation-for-the-best-results/)). For reference images, the formula is "[Reference images] + [Relationship instruction] + [New scenario]", which supports giving each attached image a labelled role. Google also endorses a workflow in which a language model writes the image prompts, which matches having Claude author them.

Practitioners add a counterweight to "be detailed": **Gemini "tends to follow the clearest, most concrete instruction it's given rather than the most detailed one"** ([Lilach Bullock](https://www.lilachbullock.com/nano-banana-prompts-copy-paste-gemini-image-edits/)). Third-party guides agree that one change per turn, with an explicit keep-list, beats compound instructions ([Leonardo.ai](https://leonardo.ai/news/nano-banana-prompt-guide)).

The rulebook practices follow from this. Name every fixed element concretely enough to identify it, for example "the red-brick two-storey house on the left" or "the large oak in the right corner". State that the camera position, framing, perspective and aspect ratio stay identical, since Google advises stating the aspect ratio when the model fails to keep it. Describe the observed light direction and time of day. Name real species suited to the confirmed hardiness zone, at realistic sizes ("as established for two years"). Ask for an unretouched photograph, not a render. Request 2K output and set the aspect ratio in the API config. Generate each view **one-shot from the original photo** with at most one to three small follow-ups. Larger changes mean returning to the original with a revised prompt, never editing the edit.

The draft per-photo template below is synthesised from that guidance and has **not been tested**:

> Using this photo of a residential back garden, redesign only the planted beds and lawn in the foreground and middle ground as a [lens, e.g. Chatto-style gravel garden]. Replace the patchy lawn with [material/planting]. Plant [3–6 named species suited to zone X, with heights and colours] in repeated groups of three, taller plants against the back fence. Add [hardscape, e.g. a 1.2 m-wide path of buff sandstone leading to a bench].
> The following stay exactly as they are in the original photo, unchanged in shape, colour, texture and position: the [house description], the [wall/fence description], the [mature tree description], the sky and all neighbouring buildings. Keep the identical camera position, lens, framing, perspective and [aspect ratio]. Match the original lighting, [time of day, light direction, overcast or sunny], with shadows falling the same way.
> The result looks like an unretouched photograph of the same garden about two years after planting, with plants at realistic sizes, not a 3D render or illustration.

Consistency across several photos comes from two mechanisms, because no seed control is documented. The first is a **canonical design brief** that Claude reuses verbatim in every prompt: lens, named plant palette, materials, colours and season. The second is an **approved render attached as a reference**, with its role spelled out, for example "Image 2 shows the approved redesign of the same garden from another angle; use the same plants, materials and style". Sources disagree on how many references help. Google allows 14, but one guide finds 3–4 the sweet spot and another says five inconsistent photos confuse the model ([UD HK](https://www.ud.hk/en/blogs/insight/article/2026-06-29-nano-banana-consistent-characters); [Futurepedia](https://www.futurepedia.io/courses/google-nano-banana-create-and-edit-in-minutes/lessons/character-cosistency-across-scenes)). Start with the original photo plus one approved view. Nano Banana 2.1's reported grounding in Google Image Search might help render named species accurately, but that is untested ([Unite.AI](https://www.unite.ai/nano-banana-2-1-debuts-at-half-the-image-cost-of-its-predecessor/)).

## Conclusion

The main finding is that photo-faithful garden redesign is no longer gated by model choice. The leading 2026 editors are close enough in quality that the pipeline around them matters more than which one is picked. A cheap model with a deterministic composite beats an expensive model left to preserve the house on its own. The user's instinct to look beyond Gemini was reasonable, but the real fix is moving from the app to the API and adding a mask and composite step. Swapping vendors, least of all to Mistral, which resells FLUX, would not address the cause.

The deeper opportunity is on the design side. The commercial apps fail because they skip everything professionals do before rendering. They don't interview the client, confirm orientation and zone, decide what stays, zone the plot or check dimensions, so they produce generic, climate-blind makeovers. Encoding the professional sequence, the eighteen checks and a short list of low-maintenance lenses lets the image model illustrate a design the homeowner can actually live with and build. That plan-first discipline, not prompt cleverness, is what will make this weekend project better than the paid apps.
