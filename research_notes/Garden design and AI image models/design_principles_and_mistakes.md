# Residential Garden Design: Core Principles and Common Amateur Mistakes (rulebook source notes)

> **Method note for the report writer:** In this session full-page fetching was blocked for every site tried: rhs.org.uk, gardensillustrated.com, homesandgardens.com, homebuilding.co.uk and nparks.gov.sg all failed DNS resolution. The findings below therefore come from search-engine result summaries and snippets of the cited pages. I did not read the full pages. Treat exact figures as "reported by the cited page"; the less authoritative ones are flagged. Wherever the RHS or the classic texts could not be reached, I say so in Gaps. Inferences are my synthesis for turning findings into rules, not sourced facts.

## 1. Structure and "bones": hardscape, evergreen framework, hedges, the winter test

### Takeaway
Designers settle layout and structure (hardscape, boundaries, evergreen framework) before choosing plants. Evergreens and woody structure carry the garden through winter. "Roughly one-third evergreen" is a common rule of thumb, but sources disagree on the share and none of them is authoritative.

### Cited Findings
- Kew-trained designer Richard Rogers: "For most designers the plants come last in the design process after the layout and structure of the garden has been decided." — [Gardens Illustrated, 10 garden design mistakes](https://www.gardensillustrated.com/garden-design/garden-design-mistakes-avoid) (quoted via search summary)
- Evergreen share: one source suggests roughly one-third evergreen structure to two-thirds seasonal planting. Others give 20–30% or as much as 60%. These are commercial blogs, so the evidence is weak. — [gardenuk.co.uk, Garden design principles](https://gardenuk.co.uk/garden-design/garden-design-principles-beginners-uk/); [gardendesignapp.ai, Evergreen plants for structure](https://gardendesignapp.ai/planting/evergreen-structure)
- Evergreens "hold the garden's shape in winter". Add grasses, seed heads and bark for off-season interest. — [gardenuk.co.uk](https://gardenuk.co.uk/garden-design/garden-design-principles-beginners-uk/)
- Border layering advice: tall structure at the back, flowering perennials in the middle, low groundcover at the front, and an evergreen in every layer for winter interest. — [plotandpatio.co.uk, How to plan a garden border](https://plotandpatio.co.uk/how-to-plan-a-garden-border-shape-height-colour/)
- A "70-30 rule" for borders appears in UK blog content. The search summary did not give its exact definition, which is usually 70% structural/repeating plants and 30% seasonal/accent. — [gardenuk.co.uk, The 70-30 rule](https://gardenuk.co.uk/garden-design/70-30-planting-rule-uk/)
- RHS prefers living boundaries (hedges) to fences for wildlife, because they act as corridors between gardens. — [RHS, Encourage wildlife to your garden](https://www.rhs.org.uk/wildlife/in-the-garden/encourage-wildlife-to-your-garden)
- Designers' common thread: plan the layout, structure and focal points first, then choose plants for size and season. — [Gardens Illustrated](https://www.gardensillustrated.com/garden-design/garden-design-mistakes-avoid); [Garden Design magazine, common mistakes](https://www.gardendesign.com/landscape-design/common-mistakes.html)

### Inferences
- Rule for the AI: design in this order. (1) Ground plane and hardscape, (2) boundaries and hedges, (3) trees and evergreen shrubs, (4) perennials, grasses and bulbs.
- Winter test (operational): mentally strip out every herbaceous plant. Does the plan still read as a composition, with defined spaces, an edge to the lawn or gravel, a framed focal point and visible evergreen masses? If not, add evergreen or woody structure or hard edges. Target about 25–35% evergreen or structural cover in borders as a default, adjusted for style; naturalistic schemes rely more on grasses and seed heads.

### Gaps
- No RHS or Society of Garden Designers figure for evergreen percentage could be retrieved. The 1/3 figure is folk wisdom from blogs.
- The term "winter test" was not found as a documented, named method. It is a widespread informal idea.

## 2. Proportion and scale relative to the house; golden ratio; house lines and grids

### Takeaway
The best-documented method is John Brookes' grid: take a module from the house (bay widths, window and door spacing, room widths), extend it over the plot, and draw the garden's shapes on that grid. This ties the garden to the building. The golden ratio is often cited, but sources are inconsistent and weak. Thomas Church's "scale" principle says parts should relate in comfortable proportion.

### Cited Findings
- Brookes' method began with a simple grid laid over each site, based on the proportions of the house or its main rooms. The grid guides the shapes that become the layout. — [Denmans Garden, John Brookes bio](https://www.denmans.org/about/john-bio-page/); [Amazon, The Essentials of Garden Design](https://www.amazon.com/Essentials-Garden-Design-John-Brookes/dp/0307269027)
- Brookes took the house module on plan and extended it beyond the building, so the garden pattern relates proportionally to the structure. This links the building's verticals with the garden's horizontals. — [Denmans](https://www.denmans.org/about/john-bio-page/); [Wikipedia, John Brookes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Brookes_(landscape_designer))
- On larger sites, Brookes doubled or trebled the basic module. — [The Galloping Gardener, winter walk with John Brookes](http://thegallopinggardener.blogspot.com/2015/01/a-winter-walk-with-john-brookes-at.html)
- Example: at The Old Rectory, Wiggonholt, Brookes used the house's 9 m-wide recessed bay to set the proportions of the terraces. — [Google Arts & Culture / Garden Museum](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/john-brookes-garden-museum/SAUBdyEKB6xdIA?hl=en)
- Brookes' grid is credited with making garden design accessible to amateurs and with unifying layouts. — [An Irish Gardener](https://anirishgardener.wordpress.com/2021/11/14/how-to-design-a-garden/)
- Thomas Church (Gardens Are for People, 1955) gave four principles: unity (house and garden as one composition), function, simplicity, and scale ("each part of the garden should relate to the other in a comfortable proportion of size"). — [Wikipedia, Thomas Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Church_(landscape_architect)); [Serenity in the Garden blog](https://serenityinthegarden.blogspot.com/2010/01/thomas-church-and-his-gardens-for.html)
- Golden ratio: one source uses 1:1.618 for layout proportions, another an odd "1:1.16" for hedges and framing. This is low-quality evidence. — [gardenuk.co.uk](https://gardenuk.co.uk/garden-design/garden-design-principles-beginners-uk/); [Key elements of garden design, Patuxent Nursery](https://patuxentnursery.com/blog/key-elements-of-garden-design/)
- Rule of thirds: divide the garden into a 3x3 grid and place focal points at the intersections. — [gardenuk.co.uk](https://gardenuk.co.uk/garden-design/garden-design-principles-beginners-uk/)
- Patio scale: one approach is to measure the house height from ground to ridge to judge how large an outdoor area should be. — reported in search summary of [HGTV, Designing paths](https://www.hgtv.com/gardening/landscaping-and-hardscaping/designing-paths-for-your-landscaping) / related results (exact source attribution uncertain)
- Main paths of 2–3 ft "depending on the scale of the house" (HGTV); a path that is too wide in a small garden looks out of proportion. — [HGTV](https://www.hgtv.com/gardening/landscaping-and-hardscaping/designing-paths-for-your-landscaping); [Homes & Gardens, path design rules](https://homesandgardens.com/gardens/garden-path-design-rules)
- Not accounting for scale and proportion in planting is called "one of the easiest" mistakes. — [Homes & Gardens, spring design mistakes](https://homesandgardens.com/gardens/common-spring-garden-design-mistakes-and-how-designers-fix-them)

### Inferences
- Rule for the AI: derive a grid module from the house photo. Usable reference lines include door and window widths, the spacing between them, the bay width, and the distance from the house corner to the door. Snap terrace edges, path alignments and bed edges to those lines and to multiples of the module. Rectangles in roughly 1:1.6 proportion, or multiples of the module, look settled. Avoid arbitrary angles that don't relate to the house.
- Scale things up: terraces, steps and paths in amateur designs are almost always under-sized relative to the house (see Section 6). Err on the generous side next to the house and get finer and smaller further away.

### Gaps
- Brookes' exact module rules, such as how many grid squares or how to pick the module, are in his books (Room Outside, 1969; Garden Design), which I couldn't access.
- No authoritative source ties the golden ratio to garden layout. Treat it as an aesthetic heuristic, not a standard.

## 3. Unity, rhythm and repetition; simplicity; limiting materials and plants

### Takeaway
Church's unity and simplicity principles, and designers' warnings against cramming, point to one rule: fewer materials, fewer species in bigger groups, and repetition of plants and groups across the garden.

### Cited Findings
- Church: "a simple layout is more beautiful and effective than a fussy one". Treat house and garden as a whole. — [Serenity in the Garden blog (Church principles)](https://serenityinthegarden.blogspot.com/2017/04/thomas-church-and-his-gardens-for-people.html); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Church_(landscape_architect))
- The main thing that trips people up in small gardens is "overcompensating for the small dimensions of your garden by trying to cram in more things". Fewer, larger, layered beds work better. — [Homes & Gardens, small garden mistakes](https://www.homesandgardens.com/gardens/small-garden-mistakes-and-how-to-fix-them)
- Group plants in threes or other odd numbers and repeat the cluster two or three times. — [Kane Landscapes, Rule of 3](https://kanelandscapes.com/blog-rule-of-3-garden-design/)
- A starter palette for matrix-style planting: 3 structural plants + 5 seasonal plants, each repeated in waves of 3 (15) + 2 matrix species = about 20 taxa. — [Plant Wild, matrix planting](https://plantwild.com/garden-design/how-to-use-matrix-planting-designs/)
- Rainer: "the more matrix-dominant the planting, the more restful the scene will be visually". — [Plant Wild](https://plantwild.com/garden-design/how-to-use-matrix-planting-designs/) / [GardenRant](https://gardenrant.com/2025/05/confused-by-matrix-design-layering-and-intermingling-ill-just-stick-with-massing-for-its-many-benefits.html)
- Designers disagree on massing versus intermingling. Benjamin Vogt and GardenRant defend massing ("I'll just stick with massing for its many benefits"). Kingsbury says intermingled plantings knit together better than single-cultivar blocks. — [GardenRant](https://gardenrant.com/2025/05/confused-by-matrix-design-layering-and-intermingling-ill-just-stick-with-massing-for-its-many-benefits.html); [Noel Kingsbury, Mind the Gap](https://www.noelkingsbury.com/noelsgarden-blog/2019/6/20/mind-the-gap)

### Inferences
- Rules for the AI:
  - Hardscape: at most 2–3 hard materials, plus 1 accent. Echo materials from the house (brick, render colour, stone).
  - Plants: for a small or medium garden, about 10–20 taxa in total, each used several times. Repeat 1–3 "signature" plants along the length of the garden to create rhythm.
  - Use one style vocabulary (formal/geometric vs. naturalistic vs. cottage). Don't mix them in one space.
- "Too many materials or plants" is a check that can be automated: count distinct paving types and distinct plant taxa per m².

### Gaps
- I found no sourced numeric cap on materials such as "max 3". This is common designer folklore I couldn't confirm in a retrievable source. Gardens Illustrated's "9 golden rules" page ([link](https://www.gardensillustrated.com/garden-design/golden-garden-design-rules)) likely covers it but could not be fetched.

## 4. Focal points, sightlines, axes, framing, borrowed landscape, hiding eyesores

### Takeaway
Every main view, especially from the house windows and back door, should end in something deliberate. Distant attractive views are "borrowed" by framing them. Eyesores are better handled by redirecting the eye than by planting a big screen right in front, which can draw attention to them.

### Cited Findings
- Without focal points, a landscape "lacks purpose and unity, becoming visually chaotic and confusing". — [Garden Design magazine, common mistakes](https://www.gardendesign.com/landscape-design/common-mistakes.html) / [Dreamlands Design](https://www.dreamlandsdesign.com/common-garden-design-mistakes-and-how-avoid-them/amp/)
- "A common landscape design mistake is to forget the view from inside the house". Consider the window views when choosing what to emphasise. — [Garden Design magazine](https://www.gardendesign.com/landscape-design/common-mistakes.html)
- Borrowed landscape: align focal points with distant views and frame those views with planting. A gap in a hedge can work as a window bringing a borrowed view into the garden. Framing views rather than simply blocking unwanted ones makes a garden feel "open yet comfortably enclosed". — [Garden Ninja, borrowed views and focal points](https://www.gardenninja.co.uk/using-borrowed-views-and-focal-points-in-garden-design/)
- Long lateral sightlines can be softened by a central tree. Diagonal views change as you move, so trees toward the centre break them up. — [Gardens Illustrated, garden privacy ideas](https://www.gardensillustrated.com/garden-design/garden-privacy-ideas)
- Eyesores: "shape sightlines, combining strategic zoning, planting, structures and materials". Planting a big tree or shrub to hide a view "often has the opposite effect of drawing the eye towards the area". Use planters or art to pull attention from utility boxes and meters. — [Gardens Illustrated, hiding eyesores](https://www.gardensillustrated.com/features/major-garden-eyesores-how-to-hide); [Landscaping Network](https://www.landscapingnetwork.com/problems/eyesores.html); [Garden Design, disguising eyesores](https://www.gardendesign.com/landscape-design/disguising-eyesores.html)
- A yew arch "encourages exploration and a sense of the 'just beyond'". — [Gardens Illustrated (eyesores/privacy features)](https://www.gardensillustrated.com/features/major-garden-eyesores-how-to-hide)
- Church preferred flexible space to rigid axes and compartments ("eschewed compartments and axial symmetry"). This contrasts with the garden-rooms tradition. — [Wikipedia, Thomas Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Church_(landscape_architect))

### Inferences
- Rules for the AI:
  - Identify 2–3 primary viewpoints: the main living-room window, the kitchen window and the back door or terrace. Each should end in a focal point (specimen tree, sculpture, bench, urn, water) placed on a line from the viewpoint, ideally one snapped to the house grid.
  - Use one strong focal point per view. Too many compete.
  - Borrow good off-site views (trees, church spire, hills) by keeping their sightline open and framing it.
  - Treat eyesores (sheds, bins, oil tanks, neighbours' windows) with a layered screen set closer to the viewer, a trellis with climbers, or a stronger focal point elsewhere. Don't put a lone specimen right against the eyesore.

### Gaps
- Russell Page's guidance (The Education of a Gardener) couldn't be retrieved. The search returned nothing usable on it.

## 5. Circulation and paths: widths, desire lines, destinations, journey, mystery and reveal

### Takeaway
Main paths should be about 1.2 m (two people side by side, wheelbarrow) and secondary paths 0.6–0.9 m, plus an allowance for plants that flop over the edges. Every path should lead somewhere, a seat or a focal point. Paths should follow the routes people actually take, and curves or partial screening can create mystery.

### Cited Findings
- Main path about 1.2 m (4 ft) is "the most consistent recommendation" (two abreast plus wheelbarrow). The two-abreast range runs from 900 mm to 1.5 m; 1,200–1,500 mm is a practical starting point. — [Surrey Oak Landscapes, How to plan a garden path](https://surreyoaklandscapes.co.uk/blog/how-to-plan-garden-path); [Outdoor Furniture Atlas, pathway width guide](https://www.outdoorfurnitureatlas.com/outdoor-pathway-width-guide); [GreatGardenPlans](https://greatgardenplans.com/how-wide-should-garden-paths-be/)
- Wheelbarrow or mower needs about 900 mm (3 ft). One source says at least 75 cm of clearance plus a buffer. With carts, go to at least 48 in (about 1.2 m). — [GardenFellas](https://gardenfellas.com/how-wide-should-a-garden-path-be/); [ForeverYard](https://foreveryard.com/what-is-the-ideal-garden-path-width/)
- Secondary paths 24–30 in (600–750 mm); foot-only paths can go as narrow as 60 cm. — [GreatGardenPlans](https://greatgardenplans.com/how-wide-should-garden-paths-be/); [Pea Gravel Calculator, walkway width](https://peagravelcalculator.com/articles/pea-gravel-walkway/)
- HGTV gives 2–3 ft for main paths "depending on the scale of the house". This is narrower than the UK consensus. — [HGTV](https://www.hgtv.com/gardening/landscaping-and-hardscaping/designing-paths-for-your-landscaping)
- Add 100–300 mm on each side for planting that spills over the edge. Ideally a path fits two side by side and is at least wheelbarrow-wide, in proportion to the garden. — [Homes & Gardens, path design rules](https://homesandgardens.com/gardens/garden-path-design-rules)
- Accessible paths: at least one access path 5 ft+ (1.5 m) wide, or 4 ft+ with 5 ft turning circles; other paths at least 3 ft. — [City of Vancouver, Community Gardens Accessibility Guidelines 2011](https://vancouver.ca/files/cov/Community-Gardens-Accessibility-Guidelines-2011.pdf)
- Curving paths guide visitors toward appealing areas and away from eyesores. — [vegogarden, hiding eyesores](https://www.vegogarden.com/blogs/academy/how-to-hide-eyesores-in-the-garden)
- Outdoor steps: comfort formula 2R + T (twice the riser plus the tread), attributed to Thomas Church's garden design book. Targets: about 650 mm (NParks Singapore, one stride); 25–27 in (about 635–685 mm). Outdoor risers 5.5–7 in (140–180 mm) with treads 12–18 in (300–450 mm). Bunnings advice: treads 280–350 mm, risers around 140 mm, which is safer when wet. NParks does not recommend a 175 mm riser outdoors. — [NParks CUGE RTN 02-2009, Steps in the landscape](https://www.nparks.gov.sg/-/media/cuge/pdf/rtn-02-2009---steps-in-the-landscape); [Garden Gate step chart](https://www.gardengatemagazine.com/64stepchart/); [Bunnings Workshop](https://www.workshop.bunnings.com.au/discussion/341331); [EngineerFix, outdoor steps](https://engineerfix.com/how-to-build-safe-and-durable-outdoor-steps-2/)

### Inferences
- Rules for the AI:
  - Main route from the house to the main destinations: at least 1.2 m clear (1.5 m for an older or mobility-impaired user, see Section 10).
  - Secondary or stepping-stone routes: 0.6–0.9 m.
  - Add 0.2–0.3 m of visual width where planting will flop over.
  - Outdoor steps: riser 120–150 mm and tread 350–450 mm, so 2R + T is about 650–700 mm. Garden steps should be shallower and deeper than indoor stairs. Keep risers consistent within a flight.
  - Every path must end at a destination (seat, terrace, gate, focal point, shed). Dead-end paths and "paths to nowhere" are errors.
  - Follow desire lines. If the photos show a worn track across the lawn, put the path there or add stepping stones.
  - Mystery and reveal: in a long plot, use a partial screen (hedge, pleached trees, a border projecting across the garden) so the whole garden can't be seen from the house. Curves should have a reason, such as going around a tree or bed; wiggly paths with no purpose are a known amateur mark.

### Gaps
- RHS official path widths couldn't be fetched. The UK figures above come from landscapers and blogs, not the RHS or SGD.
- No good source turned up on desire lines or staged reveal for residential gardens, beyond the general principles above.

## 6. Garden rooms and zoning; typical patio and seating dimensions

### Takeaway
Church's "function" principle says every area, including service areas, should have a purpose. A dining terrace for 4–6 people needs at least about 3 x 3 m (3 x 2.5 m for a rectangular table), and generous is better. Lounging, cooking and dining each need their own footprint.

### Cited Findings
- Church: all areas should have a function, "including the service (trash can, storage) area, the family use area and the entry area". — [Serenity in the Garden blog](https://serenityinthegarden.blogspot.com/2010/01/thomas-church-and-his-gardens-for.html)
- Patio for dining: 3 x 3 m is "a sensible size for a table seating four, or possibly six" (homebuilding.co.uk, via search summary). About 3 x 2.5 m for a six-seater (retailer). A US guide gives 10 ft 6 in (about 3.2 m) as the minimum for a 48 in round table. Medium patio for 4–6 diners: 3–4.9 m each way (Houzz). — [Homebuilding & Renovating, patio sizes](https://homebuilding.co.uk/advice/patio-sizes); [ConcreteNetwork, patio size](https://concretenetwork.com/patio/size.html); [Houzz, key measurements for your patio](https://www.houzz.ru/statyi/key-measurements-for-designing-your-perfect-patio-stsetivw-vs~89359139)
- Six-seat tables: rectangular 60–72 in long (1.5–1.8 m) x about 42 in (1.07 m); round 48–56 in (1.2–1.4 m). — [Edward Martin](https://www.edwardmartin.com/blogs/information/how-long-should-an-outdoor-dining-table-be); [Posh](https://posh.co.uk/living/what-is-the-best-size-patio-dining-table/)
- Pick the main uses first: dining needs more footprint than lounging, and cooking needs breathing room. — search summary of [Homes & Gardens / Fine Gardening results](https://finegardening.com/article/things-to-think-about-when-planning-or-renovating-a-garden)
- If you don't plan to spend much time outdoors with children, "you don't really need any grass lawn". — [Living Etc, landscape designer mistakes](https://www.livingetc.com/news/the-5-garden-design-mistakes-people-make-and-how-to-avoid-them)

### Inferences
- Rules for the AI:
  - Dining terrace: table footprint plus about 0.9–1.0 m clearance on every side so chairs can be pushed back and people can walk behind. For a 4–6 seat table, that means at least 3.0 x 3.0 m and ideally 3.5–4.0 m. Lounge set (sofa, two chairs, coffee table): about 3.5 x 3.5 m. Add a 0.9–1.2 m circulation strip if the main route crosses the terrace. The clearance figure is my standard furniture-planning assumption; no source found states it.
  - Zones to consider: entertaining/dining (next to the kitchen door), quiet seat (in the best evening-sun or view spot, often away from the house), play, utility (bins, compost, shed, washing line, kept near access but screened), and growing (sunny, near a tap).
  - Use grid-derived proportions so the terrace relates to the house width. A strip terrace along the house is the commonest weak choice. Consider moving the main sitting area to where the sun is at the times it gets used.

### Gaps
- No retrievable RHS or SGD guidance on clearance behind chairs; the search summary explicitly flagged this as missing.

## 7. Planting design: layering, matrix, drifts vs blocks, odd numbers, colour, texture and form, succession, right plant right place

### Takeaway
Modern planting design (Oudolf/Kingsbury, Rainer & West) layers plants into structural, seasonal-theme, filler and groundcover-matrix layers, and covers the soil densely. Perennial densities run about 5–9 per m². Group plants in odd numbers and repeat them. Colour schemes come from the colour wheel: warm colours advance, cool colours recede.

### Cited Findings
- Rainer & West's "designed plant community" has four layers: structural, seasonal theme, groundcover matrix and dynamic filler. The matrix layer works mainly as groundcover to suppress weeds. There is also a "functional layer": food and shelter for insects, weed suppression and soil protection. — [Wikipedia, Matrix planting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_planting); [Meristem Horticulture, Layer by layer](https://meristemhorticulture.com/planted/layer-by-layer)
- In Oudolf's matrix, one species or a handful dominates, and other plants are blended into it. — [Horticulture magazine, matrix planting approach](https://www.hortmag.com/gardens/matrix-planting-garden-design)
- Benjamin Vogt (not Rainer & West) suggests a 50:50 grasses-to-forbs starting ratio, with some going to 80:20. — [Plant Wild](https://plantwild.com/garden-design/how-to-use-matrix-planting-designs/)
- Density: Kingsbury says modern thinking favours about 7–9 perennials per m², and intermingled plantings knit together better. RHS-derived guidance (via calculator sites): about 5 herbaceous perennials, or 3 small shrubs, or 1 large shrub per m². STIHL suggests spacing at about 1/3 of eventual height: about 2/m² for 60–100 cm perennials, 7–9/m² for 20–40 cm fillers, up to 15/m² for groundcovers. The sources conflict (5 vs 7–9 per m²). — [Noel Kingsbury, Mind the Gap](https://www.noelkingsbury.com/noelsgarden-blog/2019/6/20/mind-the-gap); [gardencalc.uk](https://www.gardencalc.uk/planting/plant-spacing-calculator); [STIHL, planting perennials](https://www.stihl.co.uk/en/garden-ideas-and-advice/garden-maintenance/garden-bed-care/building-plant-beds/planting-perennials)
- A correctly spaced new bed looks bare at first, and the gaps close as plants grow. — [STIHL](https://www.stihl.co.uk/en/garden-ideas-and-advice/garden-maintenance/garden-bed-care/building-plant-beds/planting-perennials)
- Not planning for mature size is a common mistake that leads to moving or hard pruning. — [Garden Design magazine](https://www.gardendesign.com/landscape-design/common-mistakes.html)
- Odd numbers: clusters of 3, with the cluster repeated 2–3 times. — [Kane Landscapes](https://kanelandscapes.com/blog-rule-of-3-garden-design/)
- Colour: analogous schemes use neighbouring hues; complementary schemes pair opposites (blue + orange, magenta + chartreuse, yellow + purple), and each intensifies the other. Split-complementary is a three-colour option. — [Proven Winners ColorChoice](https://www.provenwinnerscolorchoice.com/picking-the-right-garden-color-scheme/); [Garden Design, colour](https://www.gardendesign.com/ideas/color.html); [Gingham Gardens](https://ginghamgardens.com/color-combinations-in-a-flower-garden/)
- Warm colours appear closer and cool colours recede. In a long thin garden, put hot colours near the house and cool colours at the far end. Hot colours suit sun; pastels suit shade or evening use. — [gardenuk.co.uk, Using colour](https://gardenuk.co.uk/garden-design/using-colour-garden-design-uk/)
- Only planning for spring or summer flowers, rather than four seasons, is "one of the most common mistakes". — [Homes & Gardens, spring mistakes](https://homesandgardens.com/gardens/common-spring-garden-design-mistakes-and-how-designers-fix-them)
- RHS: mix native and non-native plants, with nectar and pollen sources across the seasons. — [RHS, encourage wildlife](https://www.rhs.org.uk/wildlife/in-the-garden/encourage-wildlife-to-your-garden)

### Inferences
- Rules for the AI:
  - Layering: trees, then large shrubs or evergreen structure, then seasonal-theme perennials and grasses, then groundcover. Leave no bare soil at maturity.
  - Group herbaceous plants in odd numbers (3, 5, 7) or drifts. Avoid single dots ("one of everything").
  - Repeat key plants along the border.
  - Space plants for mature size: about 5–9/m² for medium perennials and about 1 m² per large shrub. Trees at least their mature canopy radius from the house and boundaries.
  - Choose one colour scheme per area (analogous, complementary or monochrome plus foliage green). Use cool colours and fine textures at the far end to add depth, and bold texture and hot colours near the viewer.
  - Contrast leaf form and texture (strappy, bold-round, fine-feathery), not just flower colour.
  - Succession: each bed should have interest in at least 3 seasons (bulbs, then early perennials, then summer perennials, then grasses, seed heads and evergreens in autumn and winter).
  - "Right plant, right place" (Beth Chatto's principle; I couldn't source it in this session): match plants to the aspect, sun hours and soil seen or inferred from the photos.
- Drifts vs blocks: blocks or masses read as modern and graphic and are easy to maintain. Intermingled drifts and matrix read as naturalistic and resilient, but need more skill. Default to bold repeated groups for low-maintenance clients.

### Gaps
- Primary texts (Oudolf/Kingsbury, Planting: A New Perspective; Rainer & West) couldn't be read, so their exact percentage recommendations are unverified.
- I found no sourced guidance on texture and form specifically.

## 8. Maintenance level as a design driver; low-maintenance strategies

### Takeaway
Plan for the time actually available. Dense, layered planting with groundcover, gravel gardens, fewer and repeated species, and less lawn edging all reduce work. Naturalistic and matrix planting is pitched as needing less work.

### Cited Findings
- A naturalistic garden "requires less work" and is better for the environment. — [Fine Gardening, naturalistic garden](https://www.finegardening.com/article/a-naturalistic-garden-is-better-for-the-environment-and-requires-less-work)
- The matrix or groundcover layer suppresses weeds. — [Meristem Horticulture](https://meristemhorticulture.com/planted/layer-by-layer)
- Well-planted gravel and rock gardens are low maintenance, need little watering, and attract specialised wildlife such as mason bees. — [RHS, wildlife-friendly garden](https://www.rhs.org.uk/garden-inspiration/design/wild-and-wonderful-garden)
- Massing is defended partly for its maintenance benefits. — [GardenRant](https://gardenrant.com/2025/05/confused-by-matrix-design-layering-and-intermingling-ill-just-stick-with-massing-for-its-many-benefits.html)
- A lawn edge trench at least 4 cm deep keeps the mower out of the border. — [diygarden.co.uk, creating a border](https://diygarden.co.uk/garden-design/how-to-create-a-new-border)

### Inferences
- Rules for the AI:
  - Ask for, or assume, a maintenance budget in hours per week.
  - Low-maintenance defaults: fewer, larger beds; shrubs and evergreen structure; long-lived clump-forming perennials and grasses; groundcover instead of bare soil and mulch; mowing strips (a paving edge flush with the lawn); simple lawn shapes with no tight curves or small island beds; no fussy topiary or annual bedding; automatic irrigation for containers, or few containers.
  - Hedges create trimming work. Weigh that against fences with climbers.

### Gaps
- No quantitative maintenance estimates (hours per m² per year by planting type) were found.

## 9. Sustainability and wildlife: native plants, pollinators, water, permeable surfaces, rain gardens, less lawn

### Takeaway
RHS advice: a native and non-native mix, flowering across the seasons, some unmown grass, a pond however small, hedges rather than fences, minimal and directional lighting, rain gardens and gravel gardens. In England, impermeable front-garden paving over 5 m² that doesn't drain to a permeable area needs planning permission.

### Cited Findings
- Leave some grass unmown for all or part of the year. Well-managed short grass can also benefit biodiversity (RHS Wild About Gardens 2023 lawn theme). Adding flowers to lawns boosts their value. — [RHS wildlife](https://www.rhs.org.uk/wildlife); [RHS, encourage wildlife](https://www.rhs.org.uk/wildlife/in-the-garden/encourage-wildlife-to-your-garden)
- A mix of native and non-native plants; nectar and pollen-rich plants across different times of year. — [RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/wildlife/in-the-garden/encourage-wildlife-to-your-garden)
- "The single easiest way to add wildlife value to a garden is to install a pond, however tiny." — [RHS, wildlife-friendly garden](https://www.rhs.org.uk/garden-inspiration/design/wild-and-wonderful-garden)
- A rain garden offers "a lifeline to wildlife in extremes of flood and drought". — [RHS, gardening in a changing climate](https://www.rhs.org.uk/wildlife/gardening-in-a-changing-climate)
- Keep garden and security lighting to a minimum. Use low-impact directional lighting on timers for nocturnal creatures. — [RHS wildlife pages](https://www.rhs.org.uk/wildlife/in-the-garden/encourage-wildlife-to-your-garden); [RHS community garden wildlife](https://www.rhs.org.uk/get-involved/community-gardening/resources/wildlife)
- Hedges rather than fences, as wildlife corridors. — [RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/wildlife/in-the-garden/encourage-wildlife-to-your-garden)
- Some wildlife, such as bats, isn't visible year round and is illegal to disturb. Survey before major tree work. — [RHS, mature gardens](https://www.rhs.org.uk/garden-inspiration/garden-types/mature-gardens)
- England (2008 permitted development rules): a front driveway of any size needs no planning permission if it is permeable (gravel, permeable block paving) or drains to a permeable area. Impermeable surfacing over 5 m² that doesn't drain to such an area needs permission. Wheel-track driveways are one option. Hillingdon locally suggests keeping 25% of the front garden permeable. — [UK Government, Guidance on permeable surfacing of front gardens](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a797a8eed915d07d35b5d8e/pavingfrontgardens.pdf); [Hillingdon leaflet](https://www.hillingdon.gov.uk/media/3620/a-leaflet-on-paving-front-gardens/pdf/PavingfrontgardensinformativeMay2017_2_1.pdf?m=1601985562570)

### Inferences
- Rules for the AI:
  - Default to permeable surfaces (gravel, permeable pavers, paving laid on open joints) where practical.
  - Direct downpipes to a rain garden or planted area rather than drains.
  - Include at least one water source for wildlife (small pond or bowl).
  - Replace part of the lawn with planting or meadow, but keep lawn where it's actually used. For an older client, a small, simple, well-edged lawn or none at all may be easiest.
  - Choose a long-flowering, seasonally staggered pollinator palette.
  - Lighting should be warm, low, directional and on timers.

### Gaps
- RHS rain garden sizing could not be retrieved. Common US guidance of about 10–30% of the contributing roof area is unverified here.
- The search returned nothing usable on water-wise or xeriscape plant palettes beyond gravel gardens.

## 10. Designing for older users and ageing in place

### Takeaway
Remove or minimise level changes, use firm, non-slip, level surfaces (not loose bark), wide paths with turning space, gentle ramps (1:20 preferred, 1:12 maximum), handrails on both steps and ramps, raised beds at reachable heights and widths, frequent shaded seating, and lighting for evening use.

### Cited Findings
- Ramps: 1:12 is commonly cited (1 m rise per 12 m run). One source calls 1:12 an upper limit that is too steep in practice for older people and wheelchair users and prefers 1:20. Long runs need flat landings (cited as every 750 mm of vertical rise, which seems odd and is unverified). — [Flowerpotman, garden design for the elderly](https://www.flowerpotman.com/gardening-for-the-elderly-and-disabled/garden-design-for-the-elderly/); [Old Age Solutions, design for elderly](https://www.oldagesolutions.org/design-environment/design-environment-for-elderly/); [Gardens Illustrated, slopes](https://gardensillustrated.com/garden-design/design-solutions-coping-with-slopes)
- Handrails: about 850 mm above the step nosing or ramp surface and 1 m at landings, with a grip diameter of about 45–50 mm. Extend the rail at least 1 m beyond the ramp start. Wood is warmer to the touch than metal in winter. — [Old Age Solutions](https://www.oldagesolutions.org/design-environment/design-environment-for-elderly/); [Gardening Etc, accessible garden design](https://www.gardeningetc.com/design/accessible-garden-design)
- Seating every 20–30 ft (about 6–9 m) so gardening can be paused. A rest point in full sun "isn't much of a rest", so provide shade. Seat height 45–50 cm. — [Grand Millennial, ageing in place](https://grandmillennial.net/garden-design-for-aging-in-place-layout-tools-and-safety/); [Thrive, wheelchair-friendly design](https://www.thrive.org.uk/get-gardening/wheelchair-friendly-garden-design)
- Reduce level changes, since steps and sudden grade shifts are where falls happen. Use non-slip paving, wide paths with turning places, and gradual corners. — [Gardening Etc](https://www.gardeningetc.com/design/accessible-garden-design); [CareYaya](https://www.careyaya.org/resources/blog/wheelchair-accessible-garden-designs-for-seniors)
- Paths: one access path 1.5 m+, or 1.2 m+ with 1.5 m turning circles, and others at least 0.9 m. Loose bark is "uneven & unstable – not wheel-able; tripping hazard". — [City of Vancouver Accessibility Guidelines](https://vancouver.ca/files/cov/Community-Gardens-Accessibility-Guidelines-2011.pdf). A gardening expert gives 36–48 in (0.9–1.2 m) for wheelchairs. — [Gardening Know How, accessible gardening](https://www.gardeningknowhow.com/special/accessible-gardening-ideas)
- Raised beds: about 60 cm (24 in) or more for wheelchair users (one guide). Vancouver recommends varied heights of 2–3 ft. Another source gives 70–80 cm. Tabletop beds with knee clearance let a wheelchair user pull up close. Width: about 3 ft (0.9 m) for seated gardeners reaching from one side, or two arm-lengths if reachable from both sides. — [San Diego Master Gardeners, raised beds](https://www.mastergardenerssandiego.org/schools/gardenbook/accessibility/raised_beds.html); [Vancouver](https://vancouver.ca/files/cov/Community-Gardens-Accessibility-Guidelines-2011.pdf); [Woodblocx](https://www.woodblocx.co.uk/blog/creating-wheelchair-accessible-raised-garden-beds/)
- RHS advises ramps, wide paths and raised beds to make gardens more accessible (no dimensions retrieved). — [RHS, gardening with a disability](https://www.rhs.org.uk/advice/health-and-wellbeing/gardening-with-a-disability)
- A paving overhang casts a shadow line that marks the step edge. — [Old Age Solutions / search summary](https://www.oldagesolutions.org/design-environment/design-environment-for-elderly/)
- Garden step risers: about 140 mm is "easier and safer when wet". — [Bunnings Workshop](https://www.workshop.bunnings.com.au/discussion/341331)

### Inferences
- Rules for the AI, for an older client:
  - Step-free route from the back door to the terrace, main seat, bins and washing line where possible. Ramps at 1:15–1:20 if space allows, never steeper than 1:12.
  - Where steps are unavoidable: low risers of about 100–150 mm, deep treads, consistent dimensions, a handrail on at least one side (ideally both), contrasting or lit nosings, and no single isolated steps (a known trip hazard).
  - Main path at least 1.2 m, ideally 1.5 m, with firm, level, non-slip surfaces such as sawn (not riven) paving or bound gravel. Avoid loose gravel and bark on main routes, and avoid stepping stones in lawn.
  - Raised beds 60–75 cm high for standing or perching, with wide coping (about 30 cm) to sit on, and no more than about 0.6 m reach from any edge.
  - Shaded seats with arms every 6–9 m and at destinations.
  - Path and step lighting.
  - Choose plants that don't need bending, staking or regular division. Keep thorny plants away from paths. Keep sightlines from the house open so the client can be seen and can enjoy the garden from indoors.
  - Plan for change: lay groundwork that can take a ramp later.

### Gaps
- Contrasting step edges and lighting levels weren't covered by retrievable sources. UK Approved Document M / BS 8300 would be authoritative, but I didn't retrieve them. The ramp-landing figure and the handrail heights come from secondary sources and should be checked against BS 8300 or local codes.

## 11. Common amateur mistakes and how to avoid each

### Takeaway
The recurring amateur failures are: planting before planning; skinny borders around a lawn; cramming in too many things or materials; undersized patios and paths; no focal point; ignoring the view from the house; designing for one season; ignoring mature plant size; and ignoring site conditions (sun, soil, wet areas).

### Cited Findings
- Planting before the layout and structure are decided (Richard Rogers). — [Gardens Illustrated](https://www.gardensillustrated.com/garden-design/garden-design-mistakes-avoid)
- "One of the biggest garden design mistakes is to have really skinny planting beds" (Lucy Willcox), meaning thin strips around a central lawn. — [Gardens Illustrated](https://www.gardensillustrated.com/garden-design/garden-design-mistakes-avoid) / [Living Etc](https://livingetc.com/news/the-five-garden-design-mistakes-people-make-when-it-comes-to-planting-and-how-to-avoid-them)
- Border depth: a 30 cm strip has no room for layers, and under 60 cm allows only a single row. Practical minimums are reported as 60 cm, 90 cm, or 1.2–1.5 m with shrubs. One source wants at least 1.8 m for three layers (1.2 m against a wall). The sources disagree. — [Topiary Twins, how wide should a border be](https://topiarytwins.co.uk/blogs/journal/how-wide-garden-border); [gardenuk.co.uk, mixed border](https://gardenuk.co.uk/garden-design/how-to-plan-mixed-border-uk/); [Gardenia, perennial border](https://www.gardenia.net/guide/how-to-design-a-perennial-flower-border); [plotandpatio](https://plotandpatio.co.uk/how-to-plan-a-garden-border-shape-height-colour/)
- Cramming things into a small garden; fewer, larger, layered beds are better. — [Homes & Gardens, small garden mistakes](https://www.homesandgardens.com/gardens/small-garden-mistakes-and-how-to-fix-them)
- No focal points, leading to chaos. — [Garden Design magazine](https://www.gardendesign.com/landscape-design/common-mistakes.html)
- Forgetting the view from inside the house. — [Garden Design magazine](https://www.gardendesign.com/landscape-design/common-mistakes.html)
- Only planning for spring or summer. — [Homes & Gardens](https://homesandgardens.com/gardens/common-spring-garden-design-mistakes-and-how-designers-fix-them)
- Not planning for mature size. — [Garden Design magazine](https://www.gardendesign.com/landscape-design/common-mistakes.html)
- Mismatched scale and proportion in planting. — [Homes & Gardens](https://homesandgardens.com/gardens/common-spring-garden-design-mistakes-and-how-designers-fix-them)
- Keeping a lawn that isn't needed. — [Living Etc](https://www.livingetc.com/news/the-5-garden-design-mistakes-people-make-and-how-to-avoid-them)
- Big screening plants that draw the eye to the eyesore. — [Landscaping Network](https://www.landscapingnetwork.com/problems/eyesores.html)
- Paths too wide for a small garden also look wrong (the counterpoint to "too narrow"). — [Homes & Gardens path rules](https://homesandgardens.com/gardens/garden-path-design-rules)
- Further mistake lists, not fetched but relevant: [Ideal Home, 12 mistakes](https://www.idealhome.co.uk/garden/landscaping/garden-design-mistakes); [Garden Ninja, 5 epic design fails](https://www.gardenninja.co.uk/garden-design-how-to-avoid-these-5-epic-design-fails/); [lovemoney, 31 expensive mistakes](https://www.lovemoney.com/gallerylist/113145/31-expensive-mistakes-people-often-make-when-designing-a-garden); [Gardens Illustrated, design changes not to make](https://www.gardensillustrated.com/gardens/garden-design-mistakes)
- Most planting errors can be fixed: plants can usually be dug up and moved in autumn. — [Gardens Illustrated / search summary](https://www.gardensillustrated.com/garden-design/garden-design-mistakes-avoid)

### Inferences
Checklist for the AI to run against a proposed design. Figures are defaults synthesised from the sections above.

| # | Mistake | Check / fix |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Planting first, no structure | Hardscape, boundaries and evergreen framework defined first; winter test passed (Section 1) |
| 2 | "Borders around a lawn" with skinny beds | Beds at least 1.2 m deep (1.5 m+ preferred for layered mixed borders). Bring planting into the middle of the plot or across it. Make the lawn a deliberate shape, not leftover space |
| 3 | Too many materials | 2–3 hard materials plus 1 accent, linked to the house |
| 4 | Too many plant types, "one of each" | About 10–20 taxa, in odd-number groups, repeated |
| 5 | Patio too small or in the wrong place | Dining at least 3x3 m (3.5–4 m better); put it where the sun is at the time of use |
| 6 | Paths too narrow | Main path at least 1.2 m (1.5 m for an older user) plus a spill allowance; secondary 0.6–0.9 m |
| 7 | Paths to nowhere, wiggly paths | Each path ends at a destination; curves have a reason |
| 8 | No focal point | One per main view, on a sightline from the main window or door |
| 9 | Ignoring the view from the house | Design the window views first |
| 10 | One-season garden | At least 3 seasons of interest per bed |
| 11 | Ignoring mature size (too close, or trees near the house) | Space for maturity; 5–9 perennials/m²; check tree mature spread against buildings and boundaries |
| 12 | Planted too sparsely, bare soil | Groundcover or matrix layer; denser perennial planting |
| 13 | Ignoring sun, aspect, soil, drainage | Map sun and shade from photos (orientation, shadows); right plant, right place |
| 14 | Eyesores spotlighted | Screen close to the viewer, redirect the eye |
| 15 | No lighting | Low, warm, directional path, step and terrace lighting on timers (also an ageing-in-place need) |
| 16 | Steps uncomfortable or unsafe | 2R+T of about 650–700 mm, risers at most 150 mm, consistent; handrail; no single isolated steps |
| 17 | Style mishmash | One coherent style vocabulary, consistent with the house |
| 18 | Unneeded lawn | Keep lawn only if used; otherwise use planting, gravel or meadow |

### Gaps
- Several mistakes requested in the brief, notably lack of lighting and amateur errors around ignoring sun and soil, were only supported indirectly (RHS lighting minimisation; Gardeners' World advice to observe sun and shade). No designer-authored source specifically on lighting mistakes was retrieved.

## 12. Rules for changing an existing garden: what to keep, editing vs starting over

### Takeaway
The consensus is to observe for a full year (one growing cycle) before major removals, so you learn the sun, shade, wet spots, hidden bulbs and wildlife. Deal with hazards (dangerous trees, rampant growth) straight away, and do a soil test early.

### Cited Findings
- Spend a full year spotting sun, shade and wet areas and finding what is already growing before removing or planting. Moving too quickly risks destroying bulbs and plants worth keeping. — [BBC Gardeners' World, inheriting a garden](https://www.gardenersworld.com/how-to/grow-plants/inheriting-a-garden/)
- Designer Ian Green treats one full growing cycle as the guide: "resist clearing anything you're not certain about". Urgent hazards such as a dangerous tree shouldn't wait, and routine upkeep can continue. — [Ian Green Garden Design](https://www.iangreengardendesign.co.uk/post/inherited-a-garden-here-s-why-you-shouldn-t-rush-in)
- Obvious problems, such as an out-of-control vine or a tree that needs trimming, can be dealt with before the year is up. — [Gardening Know How, mature landscaping](https://www.gardeningknowhow.com/garden-how-to/lideas/mature-landscaping)
- Not all wildlife is visible year round, and disturbing some species (e.g., bats) is illegal. — [RHS, mature gardens](https://www.rhs.org.uk/garden-inspiration/garden-types/mature-gardens)
- A soil test is cheap and informative, so do it early. — [Lord Decor, wait a year](https://www.lorddecor.com/blog/wait-a-year-before-you-touch-the-garden)

### Inferences
- Rules for the AI when redesigning from photos:
  - Inventory before you erase. Classify existing elements as keep, edit or remove. Default to keeping mature trees, good hedges, sound walls and steps, and established evergreen shrubs, since they give instant structure and maturity that take 10–20 years to replace. Keep anything a reasonable person would value, such as a healthy specimen tree or a characterful old wall.
  - Prefer editing to replacing: reshape and widen beds, crown-lift trees, cloud-prune or reduce overgrown shrubs, re-route paths along desire lines, and reuse paving.
  - Flag things the AI can't see in photos: underground services, tree protection orders or conservation areas, bulbs, drainage, soil type, and protected wildlife. Recommend the client check these.
  - For an older client, staged change costs less and disrupts less. Prioritise safety and access fixes first (steps, surfaces, rails, lighting), then structure, then planting.

### Gaps
- No source found on tree preservation orders or conservation-area rules, or on when to remove or keep specific mature trees. Local council or arborist guidance is needed.
- Brookes, Page and Oudolf texts and SGD/APLD guidance couldn't be accessed in this session, so the classic-text perspective rests on secondary summaries only. Whitelisting rhs.org.uk, gardensillustrated.com and homesandgardens.com would let someone verify the figures above against full pages.
